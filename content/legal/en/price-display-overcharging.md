# Price display rules and overcharging

## Summary: do shops have to show their prices?

Many visitors are asked for a price that seems far higher than normal, or only find out the price after eating, riding or receiving a service. Thai law requires many sellers to display prices for goods and services, and lets the authorities act against unfair pricing. In general, you should be able to see the price, or be told it, before you buy.

A high price is not automatically illegal. But hidden prices, changing the price after you agreed, and misleading prices may break the rules. This page is a draft summary, not legal advice.

## What the Price of Goods and Services Act says

The Price of Goods and Services Act B.E. 2542 lets a central committee and provincial officials control the prices of certain goods and services. One of its main tools is price display. When an official notice designates a good or service, sellers must show its price clearly enough for buyers to see. Which goods and services are covered, and exactly how the price must be shown, are TO VERIFY.

The Act also allows action against unreasonably high prices for controlled goods, and against misleading price practices. The sections, the list of controlled items and the penalties are TO VERIFY. Consumer protection law separately protects buyers against unfair or misleading sales practices; see the consumer complaints topic.

## Overcharging in practice in Phuket

Common situations include:
- seafood restaurants that charge by weight without showing the price per kilogram;
- bars that add extra charges to the bill;
- beach vendors who quote one price and charge another;
- tours that add "park fees" at the last minute.

Officials in Phuket run checks on price display, especially in high season, and complaints from visitors can lead to inspections.

The Fair Price section of this app shows sample reference ranges for common items. They help you judge whether a quote is unusual. They are not legal maximum prices, unless a row says the price is fixed by law.

## What you can do about overcharging

- Ask the price before you order or start a service, and check the menu or price board. For seafood, ask the price per kilogram and the weight before it is cooked.
- Ask for an itemised bill or a receipt.
- Take photos of the menu, the price board or the quote.
- If you are charged more than the displayed or agreed price, politely ask to pay that price and to speak to the manager.
- If that doesn't resolve it, contact the Consumer Protection Board or the Tourist Police. Use the Report Assistant to prepare a Thai summary.

Call the Consumer Protection Board on 1166 or the Tourist Police on 1155.
